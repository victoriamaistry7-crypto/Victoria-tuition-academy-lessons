What this chapter is really about
Quantitative chemistry is chemistry with numbers. You learn how to move between mass, moles, particles, 
concentration and gas volume, and then use a balanced chemical equation to calculate how much reactant is
needed or how much product is formed.
By the end of these notes, you should be able to:
 calculate molar mass from a chemical formula;
 convert between mass and moles;
 convert between moles and number of particles;
 calculate percentage composition;
 calculate concentration of a solution;
 use molar gas volume at STP;
 use mole ratios from balanced equations in stoichiometry.
The one idea to remember
MOLES are the centre of this entire topic. If you get stuck, ask: “How can I convert what I have into moles?”

1. Relative Atomic Mass and Molar Mass
The periodic table gives the relative atomic mass of each element. We use these values to calculate the
molar mass of substances.
Useful atomic masses for Grade 10
H = 1 | C = 12 | O = 16 | Na = 23 | Mg = 24 | Al = 27 | S = 32 | Cl = 35.5 | Ca = 40
How to calculate molar mass
Add the atomic masses of every atom shown in the chemical formula.
Worked Example 1: H₂O
H₂O contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
M = 2(1) + 16
M = 18 g·mol⁻¹
Worked Example 2: Ca(OH)₂
The subscript 2 outside the bracket multiplies BOTH O and H.
M = 40 + 2(16 + 1)
M = 40 + 34 = 74 g·mol⁻¹
Worked Example 3: Al₂(SO₄)₃
Count atoms carefully: 2 Al, 3 S and 12 O.
M = 2(27) + 3(32) + 12(16)
M = 342 g·mol⁻¹
Exam trap
A number outside brackets multiplies every atom inside the brackets. Miscounting atoms gives the wrong 
molar mass and can make the entire calculation wrong.
Quick Check
1. Calculate M(NaCl).
2. Calculate M(CO₂).
3. Calculate M(Mg(NO₃)₂).

2. The Mole Concept
Atoms and molecules are far too small to count one by one. Chemists therefore use a counting unit 
called the mole.
1 mol = 6.02 × 10²³ particles
This value is Avogadro’s constant, Nₐ.
The word “particles” changes depending on the substance. It may mean atoms, molecules, ions or 
formula units.
Think of it like a dozen
1 dozen = 12 objects. In the same way, 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles. A mole is simply a very large counting 
unit.
Mass Moles ↔
n = m ÷ M
n = moles (mol), m = mass (g), M = molar mass (g·mol⁻¹)
Rearrangements: m = nM and M = m ÷ n
Worked Example 4: Calculate moles from mass
Question: How many moles are in 36 g of water?
M(H₂O) = 18 g·mol⁻¹
n = m ÷ M = 36 ÷ 18
n = 2 mol
Worked Example 5: Calculate mass from moles
Question: Find the mass of 0.50 mol CO₂.
M(CO₂) = 44 g·mol⁻¹
m = nM = 0.50 × 44
m = 22 g

3. Moles and Number of Particles
N = nNₐ
N = number of particles, n = moles, Nₐ = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
To go backwards from particles to moles, use: n = N ÷ Nₐ
Worked Example 6: Moles molecules →
Question: How many molecules are in 2 mol H₂O?
N = nNₐ
N = 2(6.02 × 10²³)
N = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules
Worked Example 7: Particles moles →
Question: A sample contains 3.01 × 10²³ sodium atoms. Find the amount in moles.
n = N ÷ Nₐ
n = (3.01 × 10²³) ÷ (6.02 × 10²³)
n = 0.50 mol
The conversion chain
PARTICLES MOLES MASS ↔ ↔
Use N = nNₐ for particles and n = m ÷ M for mass. If a question gives grams and asks for particles, first go 
grams moles particles. → →
Worked Example 8: Mass particles →
Question: How many H₂O molecules are in 36 g of water?
Step 1: M(H₂O) = 18 g·mol⁻¹
Step 2: n = 36 ÷ 18 = 2 mol
Step 3: N = 2(6.02 × 10²³)
Answer: 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules
4. Percentage Composition
Percentage composition tells us what percentage of the total mass of a compound comes from a 
particular element.

% element = (mass contribution of element ÷ molar mass of
compound) × 100
Worked Example 9: Percentage oxygen in H₂O
M(H₂O) = 18
Oxygen contributes 16.
%O = (16 ÷ 18) × 100
%O = 88.9%
Worked Example 10: Percentage oxygen in CaCO₃
M(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100
Oxygen contributes 3(16) = 48.
%O = (48 ÷ 100) × 100
%O = 48%
Self-check
The percentages of all the elements in a pure compound should add up to approximately 100%. Small 
differences can happen because of rounding.
5. Concentration of Solutions
Concentration tells us how many moles of solute are present in a certain volume of solution.
c = n ÷ V
c in mol·dm⁻³, n in mol, V in dm³
Rearrangements: n = cV and V = n ÷ c
Critical unit conversion
1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Therefore, cm³ dm³: divide by 1000. Example: 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³. →
Worked Example 11: Concentration from mass

Question: 8 g NaOH is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration.
Step 1: M(NaOH) = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g·mol⁻¹
Step 2: n = m ÷ M = 8 ÷ 40 = 0.20 mol
Step 3: 500 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.50 dm³
Step 4: c = n ÷ V = 0.20 ÷ 0.50
Answer: c = 0.40 mol·dm⁻³
Worked Example 12: Concentration moles →
Question: A solution has c = 2.0 mol·dm⁻³ and V = 0.25 dm³. Find n.
n = cV
n = 2.0 × 0.25
n = 0.50 mol
Exam trap
Do not substitute cm³ directly into c = n ÷ V when concentration is required in mol·dm⁻³. Convert to dm³ first.
6. Molar Volume of Gases at STP
At standard temperature and pressure (STP), one mole of an ideal gas occupies approximately 22.4 
dm³.
V = nVₘ and n = V ÷ Vₘ
At STP: Vₘ = 22.4 dm³·mol⁻¹
Worked Example 13: Moles gas volume →
Question: What volume is occupied by 2 mol O₂ at STP?
V = nVₘ
V = 2 × 22.4
V = 44.8 dm³
Worked Example 14: Gas volume moles →
Question: How many moles are present in 11.2 dm³ of CO₂ at STP?
n = V ÷ Vₘ

7. Stoichiometry: Calculations Using Equations
Stoichiometry uses the coefficients in a balanced chemical equation as mole ratios. The equation must
be balanced before you calculate anything.
given quantity moles mole ratio required moles required → → → →
quantity
Understanding the mole ratio
For the balanced reaction:
2H₂ + O₂ 2H₂O →
The coefficient ratio is 2 : 1 : 2. This means 2 mol H₂ react with 1 mol O₂ to form 2 mol H₂O.
Very important
Use the BIG numbers in front of the formulas (coefficients) for mole ratios. Do not use the small subscripts 
inside formulas as the reaction ratio.
Worked Example 15: Moles moles →
N₂ + 3H₂ 2NH₃ →
Question: How many moles NH₃ form from 6 mol H₂?
Ratio H₂ : NH₃ = 3 : 2
n(NH₃) = 6 × (2 ÷ 3)
n(NH₃) = 4 mol

2H₂ + O₂ 2H₂O →
Question: What mass of water forms from 4 g H₂?
1. M(H₂) = 2 g·mol⁻¹
2. n(H₂) = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 mol
3. Ratio H₂ : H₂O = 2 : 2, so n(H₂O) = 2 mol
4. M(H₂O) = 18 g·mol⁻¹
5. m(H₂O) = nM = 2 × 18 = 36 g
Worked Example 17: Mass gas volume →
CaCO₃ CaO + CO₂ →
Question: What volume of CO₂ at STP forms from 200 g CaCO₃?
1. M(CaCO₃) = 100 g·mol⁻¹
2. n(CaCO₃) = 200 ÷ 100 = 2 mol
3. Ratio CaCO₃ : CO₂ = 1 : 1, so n(CO₂) = 2 mol
4. V(CO₂) = 2 × 22.4 = 44.8 dm³
