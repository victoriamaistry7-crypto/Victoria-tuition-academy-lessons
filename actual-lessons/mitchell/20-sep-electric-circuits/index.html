06 • Direction

Current and electrons
move in opposite conventions.

Exams usually use conventional current direction unless they specifically ask about electron flow.

Conventional current

Positive → negative

The historical direction used in circuit diagrams and most school calculations.

Electron flow

Negative → positive

Electrons are negatively charged and move the opposite way.

07 • Cells & energy

Cell ≠ battery.
Know the energy story.

A cell is an energy system. Several cells connected together form a battery.

chemical energy → electrical energy → output energy
Past-paper favourite: “Explain the difference between a cell and a battery” and “State the energy conversion in an electrical cell.”
Cell

One electrochemical source

Reactions inside the cell provide electrical energy.

Battery

Group of cells

Cells can be connected in series or parallel.

Lamp

Output device

Electrical energy → light + heat.

Motor

Output device

Electrical energy → kinetic energy (+ some heat/sound).

08 • Core quantities

Current, voltage,
resistance.

Do not swap these words. Exams often give correct numbers but award marks only when the scientific quantity and unit are right.

Current

Flow of charge

Measured by an ammeter. Unit: ampere (A).

Potential difference

Energy transfer per charge

Measured by a voltmeter. Unit: volt (V).

Resistance

Opposition to current

Unit: ohm (Ω). Higher resistance → smaller current if supply stays the same.

09 • Ammeter

Measure current
in the path.

An ammeter must become part of the route that the current takes, so it is connected in series.

Choose the position where an ammeter belongs.
Rule: if removing the meter would break the current path, it is in series.
10 • Voltmeter

Measure voltage
across a component.

A voltmeter is connected across the two terminals of the component, in parallel with it.

Tap the correct position on the diagram.
Exam trap: an ammeter in parallel or a voltmeter in series is incorrect.

13 • Series voltage

Voltage is shared.
Cells in series add.

For components in series, the total potential difference across the battery equals the sum across the components.

Vbattery = V1 + V2 + …
Cells in series: identical cell voltages add. Three 1.5 V cells in series provide 4.5 V.
Supply

12 V

Total across the battery.

Lamp X

7 V

Higher share.

Lamp Y

5 V

Because 7 + 5 = 12.

Exam method

Write the equation

Total = sum of parts.

14 • Series resistance

Higher resistance,
higher voltage share.

In the same series circuit, the resistor with greater resistance has the greater potential difference across it.

Rₓ > Rᵧ → Vₓ > Vᵧ
15 • Parallel circuits

More than one path.
Branches are independent.

Open one branch. The other branch still connects across the supply and can keep working.

2branches
ONbranch 1
ONbranch 2
Parallel: each branch is its own complete route between the same two supply points.
16 • Parallel voltage

Same voltage
across every branch.

Each resistor connected in parallel is across the same two supply points, so each branch has the full supply potential difference.

V₁ = V₂ = Vbattery = 9 V
17 • Parallel current

Current splits.
Then adds again.

At a junction, the total current from the battery equals the sum of the currents in the branches.

Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + …
Exam pattern: compare ammeter readings before and after a junction.

Longer conductor → higher resistance

27 • Lamp failure

A bulb blows.
Trace the path.

Do not answer from memory alone. Trace whether another complete path still exists.

Case A: series

Bulb 1 blows. Does bulb 2 remain on?

Case B: parallel

Branch 1 bulb blows. Does branch 2 remain on?

Full-mark explanation: name the circuit type + state whether a complete path remains.
Series

One break → no loop

The break is common to the whole path.

Parallel

One branch can fail

Other branches may still connect from supply to return.

28 • Fault finding

Why is the lamp off?

Tap each fault and decide whether it prevents a complete circuit.

Choose a fault.
Diagnostic method: 1) Is there a source? 2) Is the path complete? 3) Is current bypassing the output? 4) Are components connected correctly?

Open circuit

A break prevents continuous current.

Short circuit

Current takes a very low-resistance route, bypassing the intended load and causing dangerously large current.

Wrong meter

A voltmeter in series or ammeter across a component is incorrect.

Source problem

A depleted cell may not provide enough potential difference.

31 • Short circuit

Lowest-resistance path
can be dangerous.

A conductor connected directly across a source can create a short circuit: very low resistance and dangerously large current.

Safety: never deliberately short-circuit a real cell or mains supply. This interaction is conceptual only.

34 • Past-paper pattern drill

Explain using
rule + application.

Full marks often require more than a one-word answer.

Why does a lamp in another parallel branch stay on when one bulb fuses? 2 marks

1 mark: It is connected in parallel / has its own branch. 1 mark: Its branch still forms a complete path across the supply.

Why can too many appliances on one outlet be dangerous? 2 marks

1 mark: Excessive total current flows. 1 mark: Wires heat up / may cause fire or trigger protection.

Why does adding a resistor in series reduce current? 2 marks

1 mark: Total resistance increases. 1 mark: Greater resistance opposes charge flow, so current decreases for the same supply.

Why does a voltmeter go across a lamp? 2 marks

It measures the potential difference between the two terminals of the lamp, so it must connect across those two points.

2-mark explanation formula

scientific rule + apply to this diagram

Avoid vague answers such as “because it is parallel.” Explain what the parallel connection means for the path or voltage.
